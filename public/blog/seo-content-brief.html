How to Write a Content Brief Writers Actually Use

A keyword list is not a brief. Here's what actually reduces ambiguity for whoever writes the page.

Published September 2, 2026 · Updated September 13, 2026 · 6 min read

What a useful content brief actually contains

A good brief should reduce uncertainty for whoever writes the page — not add another layer of keyword-stuffing instructions. It should explain the audience, the purpose, the search intent, the important subtopics, the desired structure, and any facts or sources that need special attention. A keyword list alone is not a brief.

Start by defining the search intent

Before outlining anything, describe in one sentence what the searcher wants: to understand a concept, compare two options, learn a process, decide whether to buy something, or fix a specific problem. That sentence determines almost everything else — how detailed the page needs to be, whether it needs a comparison table, and how it should end.

Worked example brief
Keyword: best budget standing desk
Intent: commercial investigation — reader is close to buying, wants a shortlist.
Structure that follows: a short intro, a comparison table of 4–5 desks with price/weight-capacity/warranty, one paragraph per desk on who it's actually best for, and a short buying-criteria section — not a long history of standing desks, which wouldn't match the intent.

Give the writer a real outline, not just headings

Use H2s for the major questions the page must answer and H3s for supporting details underneath each one. Every heading should earn its place — if a section doesn't help resolve the reader's actual problem, cut it rather than keeping it for length.

Include the objections readers will actually raise

Readers rarely arrive with just one question. Add the follow-up objections you can anticipate — "is this too expensive," "will this work for my specific case," "what's the catch" — along with a note on what a good answer should cover. This produces a genuinely more complete page without padding it with generic filler paragraphs.

Plan internal links before writing, not after

Identify two or three related pages on your own site that would genuinely help the reader continue learning, and note the anchor text you'd use. Confirm the destination page actually exists and is current — a brief that links to a page you deleted six months ago creates work, not value.

Specify the quality checks up front

A solid brief includes reminders for the writer: which claims need a source, what images or screenshots are needed, what the meta description should emphasize, and whether the page needs structured data. Making this explicit in the brief — rather than assuming the writer will remember — is what makes the publishing process repeatable.

Use AI to draft the first pass, then edit hard

AI is genuinely useful for turning a keyword and a few constraints into a rough first-draft brief in under a minute. The editor's job is then to strip out generic sections that could apply to any topic, add requirements specific to this audience and business, and make sure the intent classification at the top is actually correct.

Frequently asked questions

Who should write the content brief — the SEO person or the writer?

Ideally whoever understands the search intent best writes the brief, and the writer who executes it should be able to ask questions and push back if something in the brief doesn't match what they're seeing in actual search results.

How detailed should a content brief be?

Detailed enough that a writer unfamiliar with the topic could produce a reasonable first draft from it — typically a page or two, not a single line of keywords.

Should a brief specify an exact word count?

A rough range is more useful than a hard target. Forcing a word count regardless of how much the topic actually needs is one of the most common causes of padded, low-value content.

The bottom line

A content brief exists to remove ambiguity, not to add another keyword checklist on top of the article itself. The intent classification and the objections section are usually the two highest-leverage parts.
